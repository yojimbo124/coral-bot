# Raising Independent, Persistent Kids

A reference for raising River, built from a Reddit parenting thread. I checked each study it cited against the published paper's details.

> [!summary] TL;DR
> - You can't manufacture a temperament, but independence, responsibility and persistence can be built.
> - Be **warm and firm** (authoritative parenting): lots of connection, plus clear expectations that you enforce consistently.
> - Give **real responsibilities** (age-appropriate chores) and **choices within limits**.
> - Let River **struggle safely**. Don't rescue too fast.
> - **Let River see you persist** at hard things, and say out loud what you're doing.
> - **Praise effort and strategy** ("you kept trying different ways"), not traits ("you're so smart").

## 1. Nature vs. nurture: what's actually in your control

**Claim in thread:** Genetics explain about 40% of individual differences in personality, which leaves plenty of room for environment.

**Source:** Vukasović, T. & Bratko, D. (2015). *Heritability of personality: A meta-analysis of behavior genetic studies.* Psychological Bulletin. [PubMed 25961374](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25961374/)
- Meta-analysis of 62 effect sizes covering more than 100,000 people of all ages.
- Heritability of personality is **about 40%**. The personality model used and gender did not change the estimate. Study design did (twin studies give higher estimates than family or adoption studies).
- ✅ The thread represents this accurately.

> [!note] Nuance worth knowing
> In behavior-genetics research, most of the non-genetic 60% usually comes from the "non-shared" environment: experiences unique to each child, such as friends, specific events and how each child is treated. Very little comes from the family environment that siblings share. So what matters most is **how you respond to River as an individual**, not a generic household formula. Temperament also shapes how River reacts to your parenting, so expect to adjust your approach.

## 2. Authoritative parenting (warm + firm)

**Claim in thread:** The research generally supports being warm and responsive while keeping clear, consistently enforced expectations.

This is the thread's overall framing. It didn't cite a specific paper for it, but it's one of the most consistently replicated findings in developmental psychology. In practice:
- High warmth: responsiveness, attention, affection.
- High structure: clear rules, predictable routines, calm follow-through.
- Contrast it with **authoritarian** parenting (firm but cold), **permissive** parenting (warm with no limits) and **uninvolved** parenting (neither).

## 3. Kids learn persistence by watching you

**Claim in thread:** Even 15-month-olds tried harder after watching an adult struggle and persist.

**Source:** Leonard, J. A., Lee, Y. & Schulz, L. E. (2017). *Infants make more attempts to achieve a goal when they see adults persist.* Science. [PubMed 28935806](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28935806/) · [MIT News write-up](https://news.mit.edu/2017/babies-try-harder-seeing-adults-0921)
- 15-month-olds saw one of three things: an adult trying repeatedly with different strategies before succeeding (**Effort**), an adult succeeding easily (**No Effort**), or no demonstration (**Baseline**).
- They were then given a new toy that looked like it should work but didn't.
- Babies in the **Effort** group tried to make the toy work **more times** than the others.
- ✅ The thread represents this accurately.

**Try this:** When you're doing something hard in front of River (assembling furniture, a stuck jar, a puzzle), don't hide the struggle. Narrate it: *"Hmm, that didn't work. Let me try another way... still not it... one more try. Got it!"*

## 4. Chores build competence

**Claim in thread:** In a longitudinal study of 9,971 children, regular chores in kindergarten were associated with greater self-competence, prosocial behavior and self-efficacy by third grade.

**Source:** White, E. M., DeBoer, M. D. & Scharf, R. J. (2019). *Associations between household chores and childhood self-competency.* Journal of Developmental & Behavioral Pediatrics, 40(3), 176–182. [PubMed 30507727](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30507727/)
- Data came from the US Early Childhood Longitudinal Study (ECLS-K:2011): 9,971 children who started kindergarten in 2010–11.
- Parents reported how often their child did chores in kindergarten. In 3rd grade, the children rated themselves on academics, peer relationships, prosocial behavior and life satisfaction.
- More frequent chores went with **higher self-perceived competence in all of those areas**, even after accounting for sex, family income and parent education.
- Kids who **rarely** did chores were more likely to score in the bottom fifth. Doing any chores at all was also linked to better **3rd-grade math scores**.
- ✅ The thread represents this accurately.

> [!warning] Caveat
> This study shows a **correlation**, not proof of cause. Families that assign chores may differ in other ways. Still, the finding matches everything else here, and chores cost nothing to try.

**Try this, by age (general guidance, not from the study):**
- **Toddler:** put toys in a bin, put clothes in the hamper, help wipe up spills.
- **Preschool:** set the table, feed a pet, match socks, water plants.
- **Early school age:** make the bed, pack their own bag, clear their dishes, help cook.

## 5. Praise effort, not intelligence

**Claim in thread:** Praising intelligence can lead to less persistence after failure than praising effort.

**Source:** Mueller, C. M. & Dweck, C. S. (1998). *Praise for intelligence can undermine children's motivation and performance.* Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 75(1), 33–52. [PubMed 9686450](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9686450/)
- Six studies, mostly with 5th graders.
- Compared with kids praised for effort, kids praised for intelligence:
  - cared more about looking good than about learning,
  - **persisted less, enjoyed the task less and performed worse after a failure**,
  - blamed failures on low ability,
  - were more likely to see intelligence as fixed.
- ✅ The thread represents this accurately.

> [!note] Nuance
> This is the foundational "growth mindset" study. Later large-scale mindset *interventions* in schools have shown smaller average effects than the early lab studies suggested. The core parenting takeaway still holds up well: praise the **process**, meaning effort, strategy and improvement.

**Swap this → for this:**
- "You're so smart!" → "You worked really hard on that."
- "You're a natural!" → "You tried a different way when the first one didn't work."
- "Good job." → "You kept going even when it was tricky."

## 6. The Incredible Years program

**Suggested by another commenter**, who said the CDC recommends it.

**What it is:** An evidence-based parenting program created by Dr. Carolyn Webster-Stratton (University of Washington). It has been delivered in more than 24 countries. [incredibleyears.com](https://www.incredibleyears.com/)
- Separate versions exist for **Babies**, **Toddlers**, **Preschool** and **School-Age** children (ages 0–12).
- **Philosophy:** warm and responsive, but consistent. Build connection through play, attention, praise and emotion coaching, then add predictable routines and clear, calm limits.
- **Core skills taught:**
  - child-directed play,
  - *persistence coaching*: describing the child's effort as it happens, e.g. "You're working so hard on that puzzle, you're not giving up",
  - academic, social and emotional coaching,
  - paying more attention to behavior you want to see,
  - handling tantrums, defiance and separations without harsh punishment.
- The goal is to build a secure relationship and teach self-regulation, not just compliance.

**The CDC link:** The [CDC references page](https://www.cdc.gov/parenting-toddlers/other-resources/references.html) belongs to the CDC's *Essentials for Parenting Toddlers*. It **cites Incredible Years research** among the parent-training studies behind the CDC's own guidance. It's less a formal endorsement than the comment implied, but it's still a strong signal.

**Cheaper alternatives to the course** (a commenter pointed out the courses are expensive and time-consuming):
- 📘 *Incredible Toddlers: A Guide and Journal of Your Toddler's Discoveries* by Carolyn Webster-Stratton covers persistence and academic coaching, routines, tantrums, biting and positive discipline. ([details](https://www.incredibleyears.com/blog/books/incredible-toddlers))
- 📘 *The Incredible Years: A Trouble-Shooting Guide for Parents of Children Aged 2–8 Years* by Carolyn Webster-Stratton is the main parent book.
- 🆓 The CDC's free [*Essentials for Parenting Toddlers*](https://www.cdc.gov/parenting-toddlers/) site, which is based on the same parent-training research.

## Daily checklist for River

- [ ] Some child-led play time today: follow River's lead, describe what River is doing, no quizzing.
- [ ] One real responsibility or chore.
- [ ] Offered a choice within a limit ("red cup or blue cup?", not "do you want to leave?").
- [ ] Let River struggle a bit before stepping in. Asked "what could you try?" first.
- [ ] Let River see me persist at something hard.
- [ ] Praised effort or strategy, specifically.
- [ ] Held a limit calmly and consistently.

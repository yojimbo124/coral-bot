# Digital Boundaries by Age

Notes for River from a Wirecutter podcast episode. Wirecutter writers Signe Brewster (kids aged 3 and 5) and Ellen Lee (kids aged 10, 16 and 18) walk through their guide to introducing tech to kids. Related: [[Raising Independent, Persistent Kids]].

Full written guide: [A Step-by-Step Tech Guide for Parents Who Live in the Real World](https://www.nytimes.com/wirecutter/reviews/how-to-introduce-technology-to-kids/)

> [!summary] TL;DR
> - **Start from values, not minutes.** Decide what you want River's days to include (outside time, play, people), then fit screens around that.
> - **Under 2: keep screens to a minimum.** Real interaction with adults is how babies learn.
> - **Under 5: you choose the when, what and how.** Let River make choices inside a "walled garden" of content you trust.
> - **Avoid apps built to hook kids** ("dark patterns").
> - **Model it.** No phone at the table at 3 if you don't want one there at 13.
> - **Teach self-soothing without screens**, so a phone doesn't become the go-to comfort later.
> - **Keep talking about tech from the start.** The habit of talking together is what carries you through phones and social media.
> - **Go easy on yourself.** Apps are designed to be hard to put down, for adults too. Screen struggles aren't a personal parenting failure.

---

## The big idea: decide what you actually want River doing

The episode quotes Mimi Ito of UC Irvine: parents spend "too much time talking about whether kids have access to a device or not, and not enough time focused on what we actually want our kids to be doing."

- Before picking a number like "30 minutes a day," ask **why** and what screen time would replace.
- If being outdoors matters to your family, **go outside first**, and screens come after.
- A Wirecutter reader survey found **only 9% of parents strongly agreed** they could confidently manage their kids' tech use. Most parents use parental controls but rated them **mediocre at best**, because kids get around them easily.

## Ages 0–5: building the foundation

These are the points the experts Signe spoke to broadly agreed on:

1. **Keep screens to a minimum before age 2.** There's little benefit, and babies learn from interacting with adults. *(This matches AAP and WHO guidance, which also make an exception for video calls with family.)*
2. **Model good habits yourself.** Kids copy what you do more than what you say.
3. **Offer alternatives.** Have books, toys, art supplies and outdoor options easy to reach, so a screen isn't the only thing to do.
4. **Build self-regulation without screens.** When River is upset, go to River and help with the feeling instead of handing over a device. This is the groundwork for a teenager who doesn't reach for a phone to soothe.
5. **Choose high-quality content.** This is the biggest single thing you can control.
   - Prefer curated apps such as **PBS Kids** over open platforms such as YouTube.
   - Check [Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/) for age ratings and reviews.
   - Watch how fast the show cuts between scenes. Fast, overstimulating shows affect young kids.
6. **Watch and play together** where you can. Media is more valuable when it's social.

### Walled gardens
Pick an app or service whose content you trust, then **let River choose within it**. River still practices making decisions, but you've set the boundaries.

### Dark patterns to watch for
A dark pattern is a design trick that pushes you to spend money, stay longer or hand over data. Young kids can't resist these, so it's on you to avoid them.
- **Play the game yourself first.**
- Warning sign: when the child stops playing, **does something bad happen** (a character dies, rewards are lost, streaks break)? That's a hook to keep them playing.
- Other signs: in-app purchase prompts, endless autoplay, and "come back tomorrow" rewards.

## Location tracking

Tracking with AirTags, smartwatches or phones is now very common. Almost every parent at Ellen's high-school info night raised a hand when asked. Many teens track their parents and friends too.

**The experts' concerns:**
- It can signal that **the world is unsafe** without it.
- It can slide into **helicopter parenting**.
- It **normalizes being monitored and having your data collected** from a very young age.
- Among teens, friends seeing each other's locations can cause **FOMO and hurt feelings** (e.g. seeing a party you weren't invited to).

**If you do track, do it well:**
- **Talk to River about it** and ask what River thinks.
- **Use it for transition periods, not forever.** Examples: the first weeks of walking to a new school, or rebuilding trust after a bad choice.
- **Build a community of trusted families nearby**, so River can roam between friends' houses and the other parents can let you know River arrived. That's tracking without a device.
- **Check what data the device collects.** Buy from a reputable brand, read the privacy settings and turn off what you don't need.

## First smartphone: readiness over age

**There's no magic age.** According to the episode, a Pew Research survey found more than half of kids have a phone by 11. In Wirecutter's survey, most 13-year-olds had one, and nearly all kids did by 15. Movements like *Wait Until 8th* ask parents to pledge to hold off, but real needs, such as commuting alone, can make an earlier phone reasonable.

**Signs River is ready:**
- Keeps track of belongings. A child who often loses jackets and water bottles will probably lose a phone.
- Shows independence and makes good choices.
- Has a real need, such as traveling alone.

**What to put in place with the first phone:**
- Clear expectations and rules, set up front.
- Teach River how to **keep the phone and accounts secure**.
- **Coach texting and group-chat manners.** Group chats can start as early as 5th grade and turn nasty fast.
- **Plan for bullying:** what to do if River is targeted, and what to do if River sees it happen to someone else.
- **Work toward River turning it off without being told.** The goal is self-regulation, not you policing it.

**Coordinate with other parents.** Agreeing on shared norms with the families of River's friends means River isn't the only kid with, or without, a phone. School phone bans help too: the school enforces the rule instead of you.

## Social media

- **What counts:** Instagram and TikTok, but also **YouTube** (comments and connecting with others) and **Pinterest**.
- **The age question:** Most platforms set 13 as the minimum, but many kids join earlier. The episode notes research **linking early social media use with later mental health problems**. That's a correlation, not proven cause.
- **Laws are changing:** Australia banned social media under 16. Opinions on whether it works are mixed; some kids reportedly moved to less-regulated corners of the internet. Other countries, including the US, are considering similar laws.
- **Schools rely on it.** In high school especially, event notices and schedule changes often go out on Instagram, so strict limits can leave a teen missing information.

**Ongoing conversations to have:**
- What is the algorithm, and why does River's feed look different from yours?
- Why is the feed endless, and why does the next video autoplay?
- If River shows interest in something dark or harmful, the feed will serve **more of it**. What will River do when that happens?
- Who does River follow and interact with?
- People post their **best moments**, not their struggles. How does River feel after seeing friends' "perfect" lives?

## Leaving home: handing over self-regulation

Ellen's approach with a daughter heading to college:
- **Turn off parental controls before they leave**, so they practice managing their own screen time while still at home.
- Give them **their own tools** to control their use:
  - **Clearspace**: an app that adds a pause, such as a deep breath or even push-ups, before opening distracting apps.
  - **The Brick**: a physical device that locks distracting apps until you tap your phone against it again (about $59–71).

## Plan for River

- [ ] Write down 3–5 family priorities (e.g. outdoor time, reading, meals together) that come before screens.
- [ ] Keep screens to a minimum under 2; video calls with family are fine.
- [ ] Put together a "walled garden" of approved apps and shows, and check new ones on Common Sense Media.
- [ ] Test any game myself for dark patterns before River plays it.
- [ ] No phones at meals, starting with me.
- [ ] When River is upset, go to River instead of reaching for a screen.
- [ ] Talk with River about tech from early on, and keep that conversation going.
- [ ] Later: decide on a phone by readiness, not age, and coordinate with other parents.
